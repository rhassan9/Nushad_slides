What is Leadership?
Leadership is a set of behaviors used to help people align their collective direction, to execute strategic plans, and to continually renew an organization- Mckinsey & Company, August 17, 2022, https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/mckinsey-explainers/what-is-leadership#/
Leadership is a process of social influence, which maximizes the efforts of others, towards the achievement of a goal- Forbes, https://www.forbes.com/sites/kevinkruse/2013/04/09/what-is-leadership/
Leadership is about empowering others to achieve things they did not think possible. Leadership is about pointing in the direction, articulating a vision of the world that does not yet exist. Then asking help from others to insure that vision happens- Simon Sinek
"A genuine leader is not a searcher for consensus but a molder of consensus." - Martin Luther King, Jr.
Types of Leadership Styles
Charismatic Leadership, when leaders uses their charm, magnetism, and emotional appeal to inspire and motivate followers.
Traditional Leadership, is based on the belief that power is bestowed on the leader, in keeping with the traditions of the past
Situational Leadership, A theory of leadership that proposes that leaders change their leadership styles based on who they are leading
Appointed Leadership, select or designated to fill an office or a position
Functional Leadership, Related to team leader theory in that they are just part of the function of the team

  Trust can be regained
‘Once it is violated, trust can be regained, but only in certain situations.
When an individual’s trust in another is broken  because the other party
failed to do what is expected of him/her, it can be restored when the
individual observes a consistent pattern of trustworthy behaviors by the
transgressor. However, when the same trustworthy behaviors occurs
with deprecation, trust  never fully recovers, even when the deceived is
given apologies or a consistent pattern of trustworthy actions’ Schwietzer
(2006) cited in Robbins 2010, p.353).

Behaviours outlining the movement of trust to mistrust
  Mistrusting groups self-destruct
The corollary to the previous principle is that when group members
mistrust each other, they repel and separate.  They peruse their own
interests rather than the group’s.  Members of distrusting groups tend to
be suspicious of each other, are constantly on guard against
exploitation and restrict communication with others in the group. These
actions tend to undermine and eventually destroy the group (Robbins
2010, p.353).
 Source: Robbins. S. P. and Judge. T. A. (2010).14th ed. Organizational Behavior. Prentice Hall. New Jersey. USA.
  Mistrust generally reduces productivity
Although we cannot say trust necessarily increases productivity, though
it usually does, mistrust almost always reduces productivity. Mistrust
focuses attention on the differences in member interests, making it
difficult for people to visualize common goals. People respond by
concealing information and secretly pursing their own interests. When
employees encounter problems, they avoid calling on others, fearing that
those others will take advantage of them. A climate of mistrust to

Rule 1 Know yourself. If you tend to
trust the wrong people, you must work on
interpreting the cues you receive. If
you’re good at recognizing cues but have
difficulty forging trusting relationships,
then you’ll have to expand your
repertoire of trust-building behaviors.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
habits, following seven basic rules’ (Kramer 2009).   /Rule 1
Source: Kramer, R. M. (2009). Rethinking Trust. Harvard Business Review.  June. pp. 69-77.USA.
Rule 2 Start small. Measured trust begins with small
acts that foster reciprocity. A good example of the
dynamic was displayed by Hewlett-Packard in the
early 1980s. Management allowed engineers to take
equipment home whenever they needed to, without
having to go through a lot of red tape. That sent a
strong signal that the company trusted employees, yet
it involved relatively little risk, because the policy was
tied to employees’ not abusing the trust.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
habits, following seven basic rules’ (Kramer 2009).   /Rule 2
Source: Kramer, R. M. (2009). Rethinking Trust. Harvard Business Review.  June. pp. 69-77.USA.
Rule 3 Write an escape clause.  With a
clearly articulated plan for disengagement,
people can trust more fully and with more
commitment. In Hollywood, scriptwriters
register their pitches with the Writers Guild – a
simple act that hedges against the risk that
others will claim a story as their own.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
habits, following seven basic rules’ (Kramer 2009).   /Rule 3
Source: Kramer, R. M. (2009). Rethinking Trust. Harvard Business Review.  June. pp. 69-77.USA.
Rule 4 Send strong signals. Most of us believe
our trustworthiness is obvious. We actually need
to signal it more clearly. By the same token, we
have to retaliate strongly when our trust is
abused. Sending weak signals about our
willingness to engage in trust or punish abuse of
it makes us more vulnerable to exploitation.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
habits, following seven basic rules’ (Kramer 2009).   /Rule 4
Source: Kramer, R. M. (2009). Rethinking Trust. Harvard Business Review.  June. pp. 69-77.USA.
Rule 5 Recognize the other person’s dilemma.
Because we worry so much about protecting
ourselves, we often forget that the people we’re
dealing with confront their own trust dilemmas and
need reassurance about whether (or how much) they
should trust us. Good relationship builders are
proactive at decreasing the anxiety and allaying the
concerns of others.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
habits, following seven basic rules’ (Kramer 2009).   /Rule 5
Source: Kramer, R. M. (2009). Rethinking Trust. Harvard Business Review.  June. pp. 69-77.USA.
Rule 6 Look at roles as well as people. A
person’s role or position can provide a guarantee
of his expertise and motivation. But be careful;
people on Main Street USA trusted people on
Wall Street for a long time because the financial
system seemed to be producing reliable results
that were the envy of the world.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
habits, following seven basic rules’ (Kramer 2009).   /Rule 6
Source: Kramer, R. M. (2009). Rethinking Trust. Harvard Business Review.  June. pp. 69-77.USA.
Rule 7 Remain vigilant and always question.
Many people whose trust is  abused do conduct
their due diligence initially. The trouble is that
they don’t keep the due diligence up-to-date,
because they find that being vigilant and
ambivalent about the people they trust is
psychologically uncomfortable.
‘To trust wisely, we need to readjust our mind-set and behavioral
