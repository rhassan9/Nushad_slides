Groups vs Teams: Core distinctions
3
A practical distinction:
GROUP
Individuals coordinate (sometimes loosely)
Accountability mainly individual
Outputs are aggregated (sum of parts)
May share information, resources, norms
Example: people in the same department
TEAM
Interdependence is designed-in (shared tasks)
Mutual accountability and shared goals
Synergy possible (whole > sum of parts)
Roles are complementary (coordination is explicit)
Example: product squad delivering a feature

Leadership styles
Transactional Leadership
Transactional leaders emphasise “hard levers of change such as designing systems and controls” (Johnson et al, 2017: 471).
The emphasis tends to be on structures, project management, setting targets, having financial incentives and monitoring performance (Johnson et al, 2017).
Armstrong (2012:14) states that “Transactional leaders trade money, jobs and security for compliance”.
At one time or another, most managers use some form of Transactual leadership because of the need to obtain results, to solve problems, to take control and to plan and organise (Tavanti, 2008).
There has been much criticism of TAL.
Yukl (1999), for example, has commented that the underlying mechanism at work in TAL was unclear and that there existed little empirical work existed examining the effects of TAL on organizations, work groups or, teams.
Furthermore, empirical support for the benefits of TAL leadership typically includes both transactional and transformational behaviours rather than exclusive transactional behaviours (e.g. Gundersen et al., 2012).
Leadership styles
Transformational Leadership
Transformational (or charismatic) leaders emphasise “building a vision for the organisation, creating an organisational identity around collective values and beliefs to support that vision and energising people to achieve it” (Johnson et al, 2017: 471).
According to Bass (1985), transactional leaders recognise what must be done to attain designated outcomes, they clarify the followers’ role, are able to meet the needs of followers, they recognise what followers want, they clarify how followers’ needs will be fulfilled and they recognise the valence of designated outcomes for followers.
The end result is to motivate followers in order to attain desired outcomes.
Leadership styles
According to Currie and Lockett (2007), although there are different views of what TFL includes, it is often stated that it is comprised of four components: charisma, inspiration, individualized consideration and intellectual stimulation.
TFL is not without its critics.
For example, van Knippenberg (2013) claim that the concept is seriously flawed whilst Alvesson and Karreman (2015) state that the key elements of TFL are arbitrary and incoherently combined.
Leadership styles
Situational Leadership
Situational leadership proposes that effective leadership requires a rational understanding of the current situation and an appropriate response, rather than having a charismatic leader with a large group of followers (McCleskey, J. (2014).
According to Johnson et al (2017: 471), Situational leaders “adjust their leadership style to the context they face”.
As with all forms of leadership theory, SAL has been criticised by a number of authors for its lack of consistency, conformity and continuity (Nicholls, 1985) and its lack of internal consistency, ambiguity and contradictions in its conception (Bass, 2008).
Leadership styles
Hersey and Blanchard’s Situational Leadership model (1982) theory rests on two basic assumptions:
1. There is no single all-purpose leadership style.
2. The leader's behaviour has two independent main components: directive behaviour and supportive behaviour.
The four quadrants with the different combinations represent the four different leadership styles: directing, coaching, supporting and delegating (Irgens, 1995).
This model is appealing and popular among practitioners but there is a consensus in the literature of a lack of empirical evidence supporting the model (Meirovich & Gu, 2015).

The term “servant leadership” was coined by Robert K. Greenleaf (1970) who referred to servant leaders as those who put the needs of others as their first priority.
Servant leaders prioritize the needs of their employees above their own, help subordinates recognise their full potential and empower them to get the job done (Linden et al, 2014).
Spears (1995 in Qiu and Dooley, 2018) stated that there are ten characteristics of the servant leader (based on the works of Greenleaf).

Heroic Leadership
Traditional notions of heroic leadership support the assumption that leadership is the task of a “gifted”, superior individual with leaders possessing distinct psychological traits and personal characteristics compared to “ordinary”, inferior individuals (Drysdale et al, 2014).

Leadership styles
Integrity is an essential quality of leadership (Tracy, 2014).
According to Leigh (2013), a leader should possess a strong “moral compass” and this will require a demonstration that ethical behaviour is a priority, clearly communicate expectations for ethical performance, practice ethical decision-making and actively support the organisation’s ethics programme.
The concept of “Authentic Leadership” has been applied to corporate ethics.
Kruse (2013), for example, notes that authentic leaders are self-aware and genuine, are mission-driven and focused on results, lead with their heart and focus on the long-term.
Leadership styles
According to Richard Barrett (2010), an effective leader will need to master seven levels of consciousness.
These are Service, Making a difference, Internal cohesion, Transformation, Self-esteem, Relationships and Survival.
Each of these levels of consciousness are supported by characteristics.
For example, the “Survival” level of consciousness with require that the leader fulfils the role of financial manager and crisis director, ensuring financial stability, organisational growth and employee health and safety.

Round 1 – Getting Started
What are you studying, and what made you choose this
course?
This initial round helps you break the ice and discover common
interests or unique perspectives among your peers.
Understanding motivations can be a great foundation for team
compatibility.

Round 2 – Group Work Experience
When working in groups, what do
you usually enjoy the most? What
do you find difficult?
Reflecting on past experiences, both
positive and challenging, offers
valuable insights into potential group
dynamics. This discussion can help
identify complementary strengths
and areas where support might be
needed.

Round 3 – Your Strengths
What is one skill or quality you have that could help a group project?
Highlighting individual strengths is crucial for effective teamwork. This round allows everyone to showcase what
they bring to the table, helping to build diverse and capable teams.

Round 4 – Time & Commitment
When do you usually have time
to work on group tasks outside
class?
Understanding availability is key to scheduling and ensuring equitable
contributions. This conversation helps manage expectations and find
compatible schedules for off-class collaboration.

Round 5 – Communication
How do you prefer to communicate in group work?
(WhatsApp, Teams, face-to-face, something else?)
Establishing preferred communication channels early can prevent
misunderstandings and improve efficiency. This round helps
ensure everyone is on the same page regarding how information
will be shared and decisions made.

Discussing expectations fosters a shared understanding of what constitutes a successful and harmonious group

Form Your Project Group
Based on the conversations you’ve had, form a group of 5 people.
Choose people you think you can work with effectively. You have
10 minutes.
This is where the insights from your speed networking pay off!
Select your teammates wisely, considering compatibility in work
style, availability, and complementary skills.
